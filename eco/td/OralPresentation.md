## Part 1: The Foundations and Dynamic of Capitalism

### SLIDE 3: Defining Capitalism vs. Market Economy

Let us begin with first principles: what exactly is capitalism?

Boltanski and Chiapello formulate a definition that is both precise and deliberately minimalist. They define global capitalism as:

> *"le capitalisme mondial entendu comme la possibilité de faire fructifier son capital par l’investissement éco-nomique ou le placement économique"*

Notice something crucial here: following the great French economic historian **Fernand Braudel**, we must establish a radical conceptual distinction between **Capitalism** and the **Market Economy**. **Capitalism is the behavior, while Market Economy is the system supporting it.**

A market economy is merely an ancient, localized mechanism of trade and exchange—it is the horizontal infrastructure where goods and services meet demand. Capitalism, by contrast, is a specific *behavior*:

Sociologically speaking, this behavior stands on **two structural pillars**:
1. **The imperative of unlimited capital accumulation pursued through formally peaceful means** (as opposed to brute conquest, piracy, or feudal pillage).
2. **The wage-earning employment system** (*le salariat*)—which means the legal, contractual subordination of human labor to the owners of capital.

### SLIDE 4: The Two Pillars & The Kantian Dove

Now, take a moment to look at that second pillar. From the perspective of pure individual rationality, **the wage-labor contract is an absurdity**. *Why would an autonomous human being voluntarily forfeit their time, surrender the surplus value of their labor, and submit to the command of a boss in exchange for a wage, while the owners reap the endless profits?*

Furthermore, as Boltanski and Chiapello point out:

> *"le capitalisme est une forme ordonnatrice de pratiques collectives parfaitement détachées de la sphère morale au sens où elle trouve sa qualité en elle-même"*

Capitalism has no internal morality. It has no intrinsic sense of justice, brotherhood, or decency. *Money generating more money.*

`[SLIDE 5: The Need for an "Esprit" and the Two Rationalities]`

This supportive atmosphere is what we call the **"Spirit of Capitalism"** (*l'esprit du capitalisme*). 

Borrowing from the sociological tradition of **Max Weber**, the spirit of capitalism is defined as the set of shared beliefs, values, and principles of justification that legitimize people’s strong commitment to the accumulation process. 

For ordinary stakeholders—and especially for managers and skilled workers—this spirit must provide three indispensable guarantees:
1. **Enthusiasm:** It must offer stimulating, exciting reasons to dedicate one’s life and energy to the firm.
2. **Security:** It must guarantee a minimum level of material security and future predictability for oneself and one's children.
3. **The Common Good:** It must offer a convincing moral justification showing that the pursuit of private profit ultimately serves the wealth and welfare of the entire community.

Here we can mobilize Weber’s famous distinction between two types of rationality:
- On the one hand, **Instrumental Rationality** (*Zweckrationalität*): calculating the most efficient, cost-effective technical means to achieve a given economic output.
- On the other hand, **Axiological or Value Rationality** (*Wertrationalität*): acting out of deep loyalty to universal ethical principles, justice, and social values.

Capitalism by itself is purely *instrumentally* rational. To legitimize itself, it must anchor its practices in *axiological* rationality. 

And how does this spirit evolve over time? It evolves through a dialectic driven by **two forms of critique**:
- **The Social Critique:** formulated by the workers’ movement, which denounces economic exploitation, misery, inequalities, and bourgeois selfishness.
- **The Artistic Critique:** rooted in bohemian and intellectual circles, which denounces alienation, the loss of personal autonomy, bureaucratic boredom, standardization, and the inauthenticity of everyday life.

As we will now see, whenever capitalism faces a crisis of legitimacy, it survives by absorbing, co-opting, and neutralizing these critiques, giving birth to a succession of historical spirits.

## Part 2: The Three Historical Spirits of Capitalism

`[SLIDE 6: Comparative Overview of the 3 Spirits]`

In their historical inquiry, Boltanski and Chiapello identify three distinct stages in the evolution of capitalism and corporate organization. Each stage corresponds to a unique corporate architecture, an idealized hero, a dominant moral code, and what they term "justification polities" or "cities" (*les cités de justification*).

Let us walk through this remarkable organizational metamorphosis.

#### 1. The First Spirit: The Bourgeois Patriarch (Late 19th Century to 1930)

`[SLIDE 7: The 1st Spirit - The Domestic & Market Cities]`

The first spirit corresponds to the early phase of industrial expansion, running from the late 19th century up to the Great Depression of the 1930s.

- **The Heroic Figure:** The central character here is the **Bourgeois Entrepreneur**—the family patriarch celebrated by Werner Sombart.
- **The Corporate Morphology:** Small workshops, family-owned factories, and early manufacturing establishments. Ownership and executive management are concentrated in the very same hands.
- **The Prevailing Morality:** The virtues of this spirit are **frugality, personal savings, probity, and the intergenerational accumulation of family patrimony**.
- **Justification Polities:** This era merges the **Domestic City** with the **Market City**. 
  - Why *Domestic*? Because the factory is managed like an extended patriarchal family. Authority is direct, personalized, and paternalistic. The patron promises protection and moral guidance to his workers, in exchange for absolute obedience.
  - The liberation it promised back then was the legal choice of one’s social estate, breaking free from feudal and aristocratic servitudes.

However, this paternalistic capitalism suffered from cronyism, vulnerability to market panics, and brutal working conditions that sparked ferocious social conflict.

#### 2. The Second Spirit: The Managerial Firm and the Fordist Pact (1930s to 1960s/70s)

`[SLIDE 8: The 2nd Spirit - Industrial & Civic Cities]`

Following the catastrophic crisis of 1929 and the Second World War, capitalism underwent a structural revolution. Family workshops were replaced by the **Classical Managerial Firm**—what Alfred Chandler famously termed the **U-Firm** (the Unitary, vertically integrated bureaucracy).

- **The Heroic Figure:** The hero is no longer the family patriarch. The new hero is the **Salaried Director, the Chief Executive, and above all, the Engineer-Manager—*le cadre*.** 
- **The Corporate Morphology:** Massive, vertically integrated bureaucratic corporations organized along Taylorist and Fayolian principles.
- **The Prevailing Morality:** Dedication to the organization, technical competence, hard work, meritocracy, and rational planning.
- **Justification Polities:** The firm anchors its legitimacy at the crossroads of the **Industrial City** (scientific efficiency, economies of scale, technological optimization) and the **Civic City** (social solidarity, collective bargaining, and national progress).

Notice how this second spirit radically repudiated the first! As Piotet highlights from page 8 of the text:

> *"What serves as a foil, as a repellent, is the logic of the domestic world and the patrimonial bourgeoisie."*

In the 1960s, family nepotism was despised. Power had to belong to those who had studied in *grandes écoles*, who possessed objective managerial science.

`[PAUSE]`

Look at the extraordinary quote cited in our plan:

> *"Hierarchy is not challenged, but it is henceforth grounded on merit and responsibility. Decentralization and Management by Objectives (MBO) foster autonomy, all the more so because this autonomy is linked to job security. The corporation is entrusted with a central role in social progress, and management serves democracy, with the Welfare State completing this arrangement in perfect harmony."* *(p. 8)*

`[EMPHASIS]` This was the golden age of the **Fordist Compromise** and the Welfare State (*l'État-providence*). Workers submitted to repetitive assembly lines, but in return, they received permanent employment contracts (*CDI*), predictable career ladders, expanding pensions, paid vacations, and institutional union representation. Autonomy and job security were mutually reinforcing.

#### 3. The Crisis of May 1968 and the Great Managerial Co-optation

`[SLIDE 9: May '68 & The Turning Point]`

Then came the turning point: the late 1960s and 1970s. 

In May 1968 in France, and across the Western world, the assembly lines ground to a halt. The Fordist corporation was hit by a double-barreled critique:
- Workers launched the **Social Critique** against grueling cadences, arbitrary factory supervisors, and wage disparities.
- But simultaneously, students, young workers, and intellectuals unleashed a ferocious **Artistic Critique**. They rejected the dull, gray, bureaucratic routine. They chanted slogans like *"Metro, Boulot, Dodo"* (Commute, Work, Sleep). They despised authoritarian managers, Taylorist division of labor, and the lack of authentic creativity.

`[PAUSE]`

Now, pay very close attention to how management reacted. Capitalist management performed an astonishing, Machiavellian maneuver of **co-optation**:
- It **embraced and recovered the Artistic Critique**! Management said: *"You hate rigid hierarchies? We will flatten them! You want creativity, flexible hours, casual dress, and teamwork? You’ve got it!"*
- But in exchange for this cultural freedom, management **systematically dismantled the protections demanded by the Social Critique**! 

The trade-off was brutal: workers were granted personal autonomy, but at the cost of collective solidarity, union power, and employment stability. This historic co-optation paved the way for the Third Spirit.

#### 4. The Third Spirit: The Network-Firm and the "Project City" (1990s to Today)

`[SLIDE 10: The 3rd Spirit - Connectionism & The Project City]`

By the 1990s, accelerated by globalization and digital information technologies, a brand-new model took over: the **Network-Firm** (*la firme-réseau*), transitioning structurally toward the **M-Firm** (Multidivisional, decentralized matrix) and lean management.

Let us read the defining description from our text:

> *"The corpus of the 1990s highlights, by contrast, the abolition of hierarchy and the substitution of market-type control for hierarchical control, the primacy of competition and permanent change, teamwork, the virtues of 'leanness', the network, and the project."* *(p. 9)*

- **The Corporate Morphology:** The heavy corporate citadel is broken up. Corporations outsource everything that is not their core competence. The firm becomes "lean" (*maigre*), existing as a shifting hub in a global web of subcontractors, temporary consultants, and freelance service providers.
- **The New Normative Polity: The Project City (*La cité par projets*):**
  In this new world, worth and social greatness are defined by **Connectionism**. 
  - Who is the **"Great Man" (*le Grand*)** in the project city? He is the hyper-mobile, flexible, adaptable individual who never stays still. He jumps enthusiastically from one project to another, building extensive social networks, generating trust, and embodying the **"Entrepreneur of the Self"**.
  - Who is the **"Small Man" (*le Petit*)**? The loser of this world is the person who is rigid, sedentary, and attached to a single craft, a permanent contract, or a stable status. In the network economy, loyalty and stability become liabilities; the stable worker is dismissed as dead weight, condemned to inemployability.

In the Third Spirit, the historic division between private life and professional life evaporates. The worker must invest their emotional subjectivity, their private contacts, and their personal personality into the market. Security is no longer guaranteed by the corporation; instead, the worker is given a new individual moral burden: maintaining their own **"employability."**

## Part 3: The Sole Trader, Platform Capitalism, and the Circumvention of Salaried Employment

`[SLIDE 11: The Advent of the Sole Trader]`

Now that we have analyzed the conceptual evolution of the network-firm, let us turn to its ultimate, real-world manifestation: the **Sole Trader**—or what in France is officially known as the **Auto-Entrepreneur** regime.

Here, our discussion connects directly with the penetrating empirical fieldwork of **Sarah Abdelnour** in *Moi, petite entreprise*.

#### 1. Legal Framework and Sociological Genesis

The *auto-entrepreneur* status was established in France by the Law on the Modernization of the Economy (LME) on August 4, 2008, entering into full force on January 1, 2009. 

It was marketed as an ultra-simplified "license to do business":
- Creation of a business in "three clicks" on the Internet without initial bureaucratic registration.
- Exemption from VAT up to specific revenue thresholds.
- A micro-fiscal option with a flat, reduced tax rate.
- Crucially: **social security contributions calculated strictly as a percentage of declared revenue**. If you earn zero euros this month, you pay zero euros in social contributions—eliminating the historical incompressible minimum contribution that protected independent craftsmen.

Today, this category represents over **7% of the active French workforce**, and continues to grow.

#### 2. A Political Offensive Against the "Welfare State of 1945"

`[SLIDE 12: Ideological Roots of the Status]`

Why was this status really created? 

Sarah Abdelnour demonstrates that this was not merely a pragmatic economic reform. It was a **deliberate political offensive against the post-war salaried social model**.

Listen carefully to the words of **François Hurel**, the author of the official government report that designed the regime, interviewed by Abdelnour in July 2009:

> *"For thirty, forty years, they made us believe—I summarize it in one simple sentence—that large cities would allow everyone to flourish better in France; that the large corporation would better employ everyone; that the large hospital would better treat everyone; that the large university would better train everyone... In short, that everything that was big was good."* *(p. 197)*

`[EMPHASIS]` Hurel and the neoliberal political circles around Secretary of State Hervé Novelli openly attacked what they called **"the unique model of the great wage-earning class of 1945"** (*le grand salariat de 1945*). They argued that collective social security and labor laws had brought France to the brink of ruin, and that the salaried contract was a rigid privilege protecting "insiders."

To replace it, public policy engineered a symbolic shift:
- In the 1970s, entrepreneurship policies targeted laid-off executives to build real firms.
- By the 2000s, state rhetoric shifted from creating a *"France of property owners"* to a **"France where everyone is an entrepreneur"** (*le tous-entrepreneurs*). 

As Abdelnour writes on page 195:

> *"A semantic and symbolic shift occurred, moving from policies encouraging property ownership to policies issuing an INJUNCTION TO AUTONOMY, reinforcing the rhetoric of individual responsibility among the working classes: ownership is less the fruit of an individual choice than it becomes an economic and moral necessity in the face of the 'crisis'."*

Autonomy is no longer an emancipatory option; it has become an aggressive, moralizing state command.

#### 3. The Reality on the Ground: Circumvention and Bogus Self-Employment

`[SLIDE 13: Bogus Self-Employment & Economic Subordination]`

When we look past the glowing managerial rhetoric of "being your own master," what is the empirical reality?

Abdelnour reveals that the regime operates primarily as a **device for the circumvention of wage-labor** (*un dispositif de contournement du salariat*). Employers—both in the private and public sectors—use the status to outsource their workforce and evade the social contributions that finance collective welfare.

This brings us to the rampant phenomenon of **Bogus Self-Employment** (*le salariat déguisé*):
- Statistical surveys show that in consulting, engineering, and IT services, **nearly 60% of auto-entrepreneurs have only one or two clients**.
- In Abdelnour’s qualitative interviews, **roughly half** of the respondents were in situations of complete economic dependency.

These workers have no business capital. Their single client dictates their hourly schedules, sets their remuneration rates unilaterally, and provides the software or physical equipment. Under labor law, this meets every definition of a salaried employment relationship! Yet, by forcing workers onto the *auto-entrepreneur* register, the client pays **zero employers' social security contributions**, owes **no severance pay**, provides **no paid sick leave**, and can terminate the contract overnight with a simple email.

`[PAUSE]`

And this is not confined to Silicon Valley gig platforms like **Uber** or **Deliveroo**. As Abdelnour uncovers, the **French State itself** routinely hires *auto-entrepreneurs* in universities, public hospitals, and municipal administrations to replace vacant civil service posts, fixed-term contracts (*CDDs*), or to re-employ retired workers under precarious terms!

#### 4. Subjective Alienation and the Gig Economy

`[SLIDE 14: The Gig Economy & The Hegemony of Work]`

What does this produce at the individual and collective levels?

At the individual level, it produces an insidious transformation of human subjectivity. Abdelnour connects this to **Michel Foucault’s** famous lectures on the *Birth of Biopolitics*, where the neoliberal subject is commanded to transform their entire life—their health, their car, their family relationships—into a **"permanent enterprise of the self."**

Furthermore, following the French philosophers **Michel Foucault** and **Gilles Deleuze**, we witness a historical transition from **Disciplinary Societies** to **Societies of Control and Self-Discipline**:
- Under the Old Fordist regime, workers were disciplined externally by the factory whistle, the punch clock, and the physical foreman.
- Under the *auto-entrepreneur* regime, there is no supervisor standing over you. **You become your own overseer and your own torturer.** Anxious about next month’s bills, workers work 60 to 70 hours a week, answering emails at 11:00 PM, unable to mentally detach from the market.

Here we must cite the brilliant sociologist **Robert Castel**, whose insights on page 201 are vital:

> *"It is precisely when labor is precarious, unprotected, and entirely surrendered to the market, that the worker is completely immersed in the order of work."*

The greatest historical conquest of the salaried contract was what Castel calls the **"liberation from the hegemony of work"**—the legal guarantee that when you clock out, work is over, and your income, healthcare, and retirement remain protected. The *auto-entrepreneur* status completely obliterates this achievement.

`[SLIDE 15: Labor Dualism & Loss of Human Capital]`

Collectively, this structural shift drives three devastating consequences:
1. **The Rise of the Gig Economy:** Millions are trapped in precarious "odd jobs" (*petits boulots*) without legal recourse or collective representation.
2. **The Dual Labor Market:** As theorized by economists Doeringer and Piore, the labor market is aggressively bifurcated into:
   - A shrinking, protected **Primary Market** of core, salaried insiders;
   - An expanding, unprotected **Secondary Market** of disposable, gig-economy outsiders.
3. **The Dramatic Loss of Human Capital:** When skilled individuals spend their productive lives constantly scrambling for short-term survival gigs, doing their own precarious administrative accounting in isolation, they are prevented from accumulating long-term, specialized, collaborative technical expertise. Society wastes its human potential on administrative precarity and survival hustling.
4. **The Defunding of Social Security:** Every euro paid in freelance invoices rather than gross wages is a direct loss for national pension funds, unemployment reserves, and public healthcare budgets—creating a vicious cycle that policymakers then use to justify further austerity.

## Conclusion: Tying It All Together

### SLIDE xX: Conclusion

To finish our presentation, let us return to our foundational question: *how does capitalism renew its legitimacy while transferring its structural risks onto ordinary workers?*

1. Capitalism is an amoral, endless process of accumulation that cannot survive without borrowing an external **"spirit"** that legitimizes voluntary subordination.
2. In the mid-20th century, the **Second Spirit** achieved social peace through the **Fordist Compromise**—offering strong job security, predictable careers, and welfare state solidarity in exchange for hierarchical obedience.
3. When the **Artistic Critique of May 1968** demanded personal freedom and an end to bureaucratic boredom, capitalism performed a brilliant historic trade-off: it granted autonomy, flexibility, and network connections (the **Third Spirit**), but systematically liquidated collective protections and stable contracts.
4. Finally, as Sarah Abdelnour demonstrates, the institutionalization of the **Sole Trader (*auto-entrepreneur*)** pushed this logic to its absolute extreme. The state and employers transformed the emancipatory dream of "being one's own boss" into an **injunction to autonomy**, legitimizing bogus self-employment, fueling the precarious gig economy, and dismantling the solidarity of the wage-earning society.

### SLIDE xX: Key Takeaways

Ultimately, the contemporary worker has indeed been liberated from the physical factory foreman. But in exchange, we have been thrust into the society of control—where each individual is tasked with becoming an "enterprise of the self," carrying the entire weight of economic risk alone upon their own shoulders.

### SLIDE xX: Text Critics

While our analysis seems pretty solid, we need to contrast it with some important warnings:

- In the analysis proposed by Françoise PIOTET, we can outline some strange bibliography choices by Boltansky & Chiapello, *which F. PIOTET will neither justify*.
	1. 
		- **The Managerialist Bias (*Le biais managérialiste*):** 
			  Boltanski and Chiapello base their entire empirical thesis on an analysis of **prescriptive management textbooks** from the 1960s and 1990s. As Piotet rightly objects, management literature consists of normative self-help books written by consultants for an elite cadre of corporate executives. Treating these prescriptive slogans as an accurate description of what actually happens on the shop floor or in ordinary offices is a massive methodological leap!
		- **A Highly Selective, Ideologically Biased Corpus:**
			  The corpus of texts analyzed by Boltanski and Chiapello is extremely narrow. They consciously selected texts that confirmed their narrative while ignoring reactionary employers' manifestos, conservative managerial factions, and the complex, heterogeneous positions of diverse labor unions.
		- **The Erasure of Material Constraints and Labor Sociology:**
			  Their approach is deeply idealist—it assumes that philosophical ideas, justification polities, and artistic critiques are what move the economic world. In doing so, they neglect the hard, material variables of economics and industrial relations: demographic pressures, international raw material prices, real power balances, and institutional collective bargaining (as analyzed by sociologists like Jean-Daniel Reynaud).
		- **The Glaring Blind Spot of the State:**
			  Boltanski and Chiapello portray the development of capitalism almost entirely as an autonomous dialogue between managers and social critics. They completely overlook the central, coercive role of the State, labor legislation, and institutional regulations in structuring economic markets.
		- **The Theoretical Paradox:**
			  Piotet highlights a stunning paradox on page 266: in the final part of their book, when Boltanski and Chiapello propose solutions to tame the precarity of the project city (such as universal basic income or portable activity contracts), their proposed reforms would ultimately **abolish the wage-earning contract** (*le salariat*)—the very pillar that they declared was a non-negotiable constitutive element of capitalism in their opening chapter!
	2. 
		- **Over-focusing on the Dark Side of the Status:**
		  Abdelnour’s text focuses almost exclusively on precarity, bogus self-employment, and corporate exploitation. While these dynamics are undeniably real, the article tends to minimize the genuine, subjective desire for autonomy experienced by many individuals. 	- **Sampling Boundaries:**
		  Her qualitative interview sample focuses heavily on specific urban service sectors (transport, consulting, communications), which may over-represent the most acute forms of platform dependence and bogus self-employment compared to rural or traditional artisanal activities.
